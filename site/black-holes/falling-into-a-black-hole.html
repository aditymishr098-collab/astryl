Falling into a black hole

The same fall looks very different to you and to someone watching from far away.

Stretching

Gravity pulls harder on the part of you that is closer. Falling feet first, your feet are pulled harder than your head, and you are stretched. Scientists call it spaghettification.

Near a black hole of about 10 Suns the stretching is deadly well before the horizon. Near a supermassive black hole like Sagittarius A* the horizon is so large that the stretching there is gentle: you could cross it without feeling anything special, and be torn apart only later, deeper inside.

Time

Seen from far away, a clock falling in ticks slower and slower, and its light turns redder and dimmer. The watcher never sees it cross the horizon. For the person falling, time feels normal and crossing the horizon is unremarkable. Both are true: time is not the same for everyone.

A trip in five steps

1

Approach

Light from behind the hole is bent. The sky looks warped around you.

2

Stretching

For a small black hole you are already being pulled apart here.

3

The horizon

You cross. No bump, no wall. But there is no way back, and no message can reach the outside.

4

Inside

Every direction you can move points toward the centre, like time itself flowing that way.

5

The centre

For Sagittarius A* the trip from the horizon to the centre takes about a minute. For a 10-Sun black hole it takes less than a thousandth of a second.

This is what the equations of general relativity say. Nobody has ever gone near a black hole, and what really happens at the very centre is an open question.
