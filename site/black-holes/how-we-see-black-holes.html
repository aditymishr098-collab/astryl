How we see the invisible

A black hole gives off no light, so astronomers look for what it does to everything around it.

Hot gas

Gas that spirals in forms an accretion disk. Friction heats it to millions of degrees, so it glows brightly in X-rays. Around the biggest black holes this glow makes a quasar, which can outshine a whole galaxy.

Jets

Magnetic fields can launch some of the gas out in two narrow beams, along the spin axis, at nearly the speed of light. The jet of M87* is thousands of light-years long.

Stars that dance

For more than 25 years, astronomers tracked stars orbiting an invisible point at the centre of our Galaxy. Their paths showed something with 4.3 million Suns of mass in a tiny space. That is Sagittarius A*.

Bent light

Gravity bends light, so a black hole can act like a lens and distort or magnify what is behind it. This gravitational lensing also helps find hidden masses.

Ripples in space

When two black holes merge, they shake space itself. Those gravitational waves reach Earth as a stretch smaller than a proton. Detectors like LIGO and Virgo measure it. The first was heard in 2015, and many more since.

The first pictures

The Event Horizon Telescope links radio telescopes around the world so they work like one telescope as big as Earth. It pictured M87* in 2019 and Sagittarius A* in 2022.

What the pictures show is not the black hole itself but its dark shadow and the ring of glowing gas around it. The orange colour is added to show how bright each part is.
